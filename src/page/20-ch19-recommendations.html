Build where power, water and fibre are planned together

The Central Electricity Authority expects about 26 GW of new data-centre demand by 2031–32, over fourteen times what runs today. If it lands where today's capacity sits, it lands on the most stressed grids and aquifers in the country. This chapter sets out where to build, what to build, and the rules that would make both happen.

Rule 1 · Cloud and colocation stay near usersInteractive traffic and interconnection need low latency and cable landings. Sustainability must come from design, because the location cannot change.

Navi Mumbai · Pune · Chennai · Hyderabad · NCR · Bengaluru periphery · Kolkata
  • Zero-freshwater cooling: closed-loop liquid + dry coolers
  • Treated sewage water (TSE) for anything that evaporates
  • Multi-storey where land is scarce; plinths above flood level

Rule 2 · AI training goes where power isTraining traffic is bulk and latency-tolerant, so trade latency for renewable power, land, water and low hazard.

Vizag · Jamnagar · Kurnool–Anantapur · Tumakuru–Chitradurga · Telangana interior · western Rajasthan · Bhubaneswar–Paradip
  • Co-locate with solar + wind + batteries at a 400 kV pooling station
  • Desalinated or treated water only; seismic Zone II/III
  • Already happening: Google Vizag (~1 GW), Meta–Reliance Jamnagar (168 MW, desalinated seawater)

Archetype

With the strict latency rule on, Hyderabad and Bengaluru drop out of the cloud ranking; they still work as second availability zones paired with a cable-landing metro. Switch the rule off and Jamnagar tops the cloud list on power and land alone, which is why latency is treated as a filter, not just a weight. No metro scores well on water, so in Mumbai, Chennai, Bengaluru and NCR water performance has to be designed in. Vizag and Jamnagar lead for AI because renewable power, land and seawater coincide there, but both carry cyclone risk that must be engineered for.

Cooling by climateRaise the water temperature so dry coolers can reject heat without evaporation; keep evaporation for peak hours, on treated or desalinated water. City-by-city systems with costs are in 6.5; every cooling tool is mapped in Chapter 16.

Zero on-site water only helps overall when it comes with clean power. A dry design that adds 0.03–0.05 to PUE adds ~0.1–0.15 L/kWh of coal-plant water on today's grid (MoEFCC allows plants 3.0–3.5 L/kWh, and a CSE survey found half of freshwater plants over the cap).

Water: a hierarchy, sized to the peak dayUse the first source that works; never the last.

Size storage and treatment for April–May, when adiabatic systems use the most (chapter 8), with ≥7 days' storage for TSE-fed sites. Report peak-day withdrawal as well as the annual average.

Hazards and resilienceDesign to the stricter of today's code and the most recent hazard evidence.

Heat reuse in a hot countrySpace heating is irrelevant in most of India; target cooling and process heat instead.

Germany's reuse quotas (10% rising to 20%) do not transfer. A workable Indian target: reuse ≥5% where an industrial offtaker exists, and a feasibility study for every site of 10 MW or more.

The short version

  • Within 12 months: notify the national policy with a single window; add WUE and hourly clean power to IndiaAI tenders; publish the data-sovereignty negative list before May 2027; open allocation records to the public.
  • Within 24 months: mandatory assured disclosure to a national registry; large-load grid rules with study fees and flexibility; 24×7 clean-power products and harmonised banking; a statutory water hierarchy.
  • By 2030: PUE floors with a glide path (new builds ≤1.25–1.30, AI ≤1.20); green data-centre zones with substations, treated water and fibre built ahead of demand; incentives paid only for verified results, with sunset clauses.
  • Throughout: make government cloud the most transparent, not the least; review incentives every three years and drop those that cannot show results.
