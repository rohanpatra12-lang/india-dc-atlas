18Research + model · Demand forecasts, NITI Aayog outlook and a 60% renewable target

Can India's data centres run on 60% renewable power by 2030?

Yes, if 60% is counted over the year and demand follows the market forecasts: about 21 TWh of renewable power, or 5.4 GW of solar and 3.4 GW of wind bought through open access, at or below today's commercial grid tariff. Counting it hour by hour costs more and needs round-the-clock contracts or batteries. And if demand follows the government's own load projection rather than the market's, the limit becomes wind, storage and transmission, not money. How fast demand grows matters more than how much renewable power India can build.

The rules that decide the costCharges and limits that apply to buying renewable power directly.

  • Interstate transmission charges (~₹1–2/kWh) return in 25% steps for solar and wind commissioned after June 2025, in full from July 2028. Storage paired with renewables keeps its waiver to June 2028; a CERC draft would extend it.
  • Banking: at least 30% of monthly consumption may be banked; states are moving to an 8% in-kind charge with monthly settlement (Tamil Nadu from Oct 2025, UP). Gujarat charges ₹1.50/kWh (draft ₹1).
  • Tamil Nadu: no banking for third-party open access; unused banked power bought back at 75% of the tariff, which favours captive or group-captive structures.
  • Grid comparison: Maharashtra's commercial high-tension tariff is ₹8.72/kWh for FY2026-27 plus ₹565/kVA a month, so firm green power is at or below grid parity for large users.
  • Carbon market: data centres are not obligated under India's carbon credit trading scheme, so certificates and voluntary offsets remain the only no-cost instruments.

What to do with it

  • Plan for three demand bands: market (31–60 TWh in 2030), NITI (~90 TWh) and government load (140–175 TWh by 2031-32). Label every number as IT or grid MW.
  • Report hourly as well as annual. A 60% annual claim built on solar and banking can be 40% hour by hour. Operators should publish hourly carbon-free energy alongside the annual share.
  • Lock in open-access capacity before July 2028, when interstate transmission charges return in full; add round-the-clock contracts for hourly quality.
  • Create a data-centre green tariff so distribution companies can pass through SECI-style round-the-clock power, as Maharashtra's 2,500 MW purchase does.
  • Build wind, storage and transmission where the queue is. The ~17 GW of connection applications should drive renewable zones in Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Gujarat and Maharashtra. In the Ministry of Power case data centres would need about half of India's record yearly wind build.
  • Measure first. The Ministry of Power says data-centre consumption is "not centrally maintained"; mandate disclosure through BEE or CEA. Every 0.1 off PUE in the central case saves about 2.9 TWh a year.

India's carbon market, and why data centres are outside itThe Carbon Credit Trading Scheme caps emission intensity in heavy industry; data centres are not covered.

  • Compliance obligations apply from FY2025-26 to seven sectors: aluminium, cement, chlor-alkali, and pulp and paper (targets notified October 2025); petroleum refining, petrochemicals and textiles (January 2026). Iron and steel and fertilisers are still pending.
  • Targets are emission intensities against a FY2023-24 baseline; entities that beat them earn Carbon Credit Certificates, traded on the power exchanges from about mid-2026. No market price exists yet.
  • Data centres buy electricity rather than burn fuel, so their emissions are indirect and outside the scheme. Their lever is the power they contract: round-the-clock clean power (this chapter) cuts the carbon the scheme cannot see. The voluntary "offset" route of the scheme should not be counted against a data centre's Scope 2.
