Water, direct and indirect

The questionHow much water does a data centre use, and how does it compare with other industries?

The short answerA 100 MW AI campus on cooling towers evaporates about 3.4 million litres a day on site (2.8–4.5 by climate) and about 6 million more at power stations (4.7–7.2 across the India grid range): 9.3 MLD in all, less than a steel plant or a chip fab, but concentrated in water-stressed cities.

Withdrawal, consumption and scarcity are three different numbers

Withdrawal is what you draw. Consumption is what never comes back, mostly evaporation. Scarcity weighting asks how much that loss hurts where it happens. A 50 MW evaporative site is 0.09% of city supply, but about 4% of Bangalore East's sustainable groundwater.

Water you can pictureTurn million litres into people, tanker trucks and swimming pools, then test the site against a 2024-style summer.

Stress test: a 2024-style summerIn March 2024, 6,997 of Bengaluru's 14,781 public borewells failed after a weak 2023 monsoon. BWSSB banned non-essential use of potable water, tanker prices surged until the authorities capped them, and groundwater extraction in Bengaluru Urban rose from about 600 MLD in 2020 to 1,457 MLD. Evaporative cooling peaks in April–May at about 1.3× its average, exactly when supply is tightest.

Assumptions estimate: 135 litres per person per day, 12,000-litre tankers, 2.5 million-litre Olympic pools. BWSSB delivers about 1,935 MLD. A borewell failure share of 47% is the March 2024 figure.

The cooling system sets a site's own water bill. The grid sets most of its total water, because Indian coal plants evaporate about 3 litres for every kWh they deliver. On site, the trade-off is real: air-cooled chillers use no water but more power. Count the power plants' water too, and the boxes reorder. Each extra 0.1 of PUE costs about 0.3 L/kWh of coal-plant water, so a 1.6-PUE "waterless" site can use more water than a 1.2-PUE adiabatic one.

How this is calculated · hourly profile
  1. Temperature: Report 12 May design day, 27 °C at 05:00 to 40 °C at 15:00 (2-hourly points, interpolated)
  2. Adiabatic: PUE(T) = 1.22 + 0.015 × max(0, T − 25); WUE(T) = 0 below 26 °C, else min(1.6, 0.1 + 0.107 × (T − 26))
  3. Cooling towers: WUE(T) = 1.9 + 0.4 × (T − 27) ÷ 13; PUE(T) = 1.30 + 0.10 × (T − 27) ÷ 13
  4. Dry + liquid: WUE = 0.03; PUE(T) = 1.15 + 0.008 × max(0, T − 25)
  5. Water (kL/h) = IT MW × WUE; facility MW = IT MW × PUE
  6. Dry at noon: inside the window WUE = 0 and PUE + penalty; extra cost = extra MWh × exchange price
Assumptions
  • Adiabatic functions and design day are Report 12 models; they give 905 kL and 1,358 MWh for the day.
  • Tower and dry curves are illustrative [model], tuned to the Report 12 ranges (towers nearly flat at 1.9–2.3 L/kWh; dry ≈ 0).
  • Exchange prices are shaped to June 2026 IEX hourly averages (trough ₹1.17 at 11:00, peak ₹9.90 at 22:30) [reported, shape is a proxy].
  • The +0.10 PUE "dry" penalty is an assumption for running chillers with pads off.
  • Report 12 says an 11:00–16:00 window halves the peak hour; on its own profile the 16:00–17:00 hours stay hot, so a window to about 18:00 is needed to cut the peak substantially.

How the capacities are found: the worked checkThe capacities are not set by a formula. An hourly optimisation chooses them, so that the 65 MW load is met in all 8,760 hours at the lowest cost, under a cap on water. The optimisation itself, in plain words
  1. It decides four sizes (solar MW, wind MW, hydro contract MW up to 40, battery MWh) and, for each of the 8,760 hours, how much hydro, grid, battery charge and discharge, and curtailment to use: 52,564 numbers in all.
  2. Every hour solar MW × that hour's solar profile + wind MW × wind profile + hydro + battery discharge + grid − battery charge − curtailment must equal 65 MW.
  3. The battery loses 12% of what passes through it, can never hold more than its size, can charge or discharge at most a quarter of its size per hour (a 4-hour unit), and ends the year as full as it started.
  4. Hydro cannot exceed its contract in any hour, or the reservoir's monthly allowance (45% of the contract in January, up to 85% in August).
  5. It minimises the total annual cost from step 1. For the cheapest option there is no water limit; for the others, total water must stay below a cap set between the minimum possible water and the cheapest option's water.
  6. The answer is whatever mix meets every hour at the least cost. Capacities rise or fall together: more solar is only worth adding if the battery can store its midday surplus, and a bigger battery is only worth it if there is surplus to store.

Inside a cooling towerWhere each litre of make-up water goes. Raising cycles of concentration (cleaner water, softening or RO) is the cheapest saving at existing tower sites.

How this is calculated · tower water balance
  1. Heat rejected Q = 1.25 kWh of heat per kWh IT (chiller COP 5–7)
  2. Evaporation E = Q × latent share × 3,600 ÷ 2,430 kJ/kg
  3. Blowdown B = E ÷ (CoC − 1)
  4. Drift D = 0.002 L per kWh of heat
  5. WUE = E + B + D per kWh IT
Assumptions
  • Latent heat at ~30 °C; latent share 0.75–0.95 (default 0.85) [proxy].
  • NTT DATA's evaporative sites reported 1.42–2.93 L/kWh in FY22–24 [reported].

What the numbers say to do

  • Buy power before choosing cooling. Above 20 MW, group-captive round-the-clock renewables are the cheapest option in two of three worked examples and cut total water by 70–85%.
  • Size water to the peak day. Adiabatic sites in Hyderabad and Delhi need supply and storage for about twice the average day.
  • Run dry at noon when water is short. The extra electricity costs little at midday exchange prices.
  • Coastal cities: avoid freshwater towers in Chennai, Mumbai, Kolkata and Vizag; use treated sewage, seawater, or liquid cooling with dry coolers.
  • Get industrial tariff status. In Maharashtra the commercial category costs about ₹1.78/kVAh more.
  • Report both waters. Publish withdrawal and consumption, on-site and grid, as Karnataka's 2026 policy now asks.
8.3Research · AI data centres: anatomy, capacity and water, compared with factories

How thirsty is an AI data centre next to a steel plant?

A 100 MW AI campus cooled by towers evaporates about 3.4 million litres a day on site. Its electricity costs another 4.7–7.2 million litres a day at power stations (India grid, 2.3–3.5 L/kWh). That is less than a coal plant, a steel plant, a paper mill or the Tata–PSMC chip fab, and close to a large cement plant. The risk is not how much water each campus uses, but where they are being built (water-stressed metros) and how fast they are being added. This chapter sets out what is inside an AI data centre, how much capacity India is forecast to build, where its water goes, and how it compares with other industry.

Direct, indirect, embodiedThe world's data centres, 2023, billion litres consumed.

  • Direct: evaporated and drained by cooling on site. Set by the cooling type and climate.
  • Indirect: evaporated at power stations to make the electricity. Set by the grid mix: ~5 L/kWh for the India grid (atlas assumption for coal with towers), ~0.1 for solar.
  • Embodied: used to make the chips and servers (ultrapure water at fabs).
  • The IEA projects ~1,200 billion litres by 2030. A Patterns (Cell) study argues indirect water is undercounted: at 3.4 L/kWh it alone would be ~1,225 billion litres.

Water stress where the campuses goThe intensity is moderate; the location is not.

  • Bengaluru: groundwater extraction at 177% of recharge (CGWB 2025); the city draws ~2,000 MLD from the Cauvery against demand of up to 3,000 MLD.
  • Hyderabad: drinking and industrial shortfall projected at 870–909 MLD by 2027–28; the state policy promises "continuous water supply for heavy duty cooling".
  • India: more than half of ~271 data centres sit in water-stressed regions (WRI India); S&P expects 60–80% to face high stress this decade.
  • Policy: no national data-centre policy and no binding WUE rule. Only 5 of 15 state policies mention sustainability; only Rajasthan's (2025) requires recycling, zero liquid discharge and recharge.

What to do with it

  • Judge sites by basin, not by litres per MW. A 100 MW campus is smaller than a cement plant, but ten of them in one stressed city is a new town's worth of demand. States should set a basin water budget covering fabs, data centres and power plants together (Narmada for Dholera, Vizag, Bengaluru Urban).
  • Mandate the water source. Any evaporative cooling should run on treated wastewater; in CGWA over-exploited blocks, only dry or closed-loop cooling should be allowed.
  • Count the power-station water. At the India grid's 2.9 L/kWh clean power saves more water than any cooling choice, and dry cooling still beats towers on total water.
  • Disclose by site. Add site-level WUE, withdrawal and consumption to BRSR: no Indian operator publishes a WUE today.
  • Report forecasts with their units. Every capacity number should say IT, facility or grid MW, and operating, under construction or announced.
  • Do not cite the 150 billion litre figure as fact. Show it beside a bottom-up estimate and say they conflict.
  • Gaps: forecasts from Goldman Sachs, McKinsey, BNEF, ICRA and CRISIL, and water benchmarks for beverages, refineries and pharma, were not checked against primary sources.
