Why build AI data centres in India, what they pay back, and what matters

The questionDoes India need much more AI data-centre capacity? What does the country get in return, what happens if it is not built, and what decides whether a project helps or hurts?

The short answerYes, but on conditions. India hosts ~1.2 W of data-centre capacity per person against ~50 W in the US, must run payments, health and government AI at home, and is exposed to foreign compute and export controls. The payback is large in capital, construction and strategic capability, and small in permanent jobs (~0.2–2.6 per MW). Not building moves the demand abroad rather than removing it; building badly strands GPUs and strains power and water. How and where capacity is built matters more than how much.

What other hosts learnedFrom courting data centres to conditioning them.

  • Ireland: data centres used 22% of metered electricity in 2024 (5% in 2015), more than all urban homes; Dublin connections paused from 2022; since Dec 2025 new sites must bring matching generation or storage.
  • Singapore: moratorium 2019–22; new capacity only by tender at PUE ≤1.25 and ≥50% green power; a bill to license every site of 3 MW+.
  • Johor: 10 MW (2021) to 5.7 GW planned; data-centre water tariff RM5.33/m³; reclaimed water mandated; water-cooled expansions deferred; ~30% of applications rejected; actual draw ~55% of approved, so operators must use 85% of booked power.
  • Virginia: real construction benefits and local taxes, against the state's largest tax break and household bills that could rise US$14–37 a month by 2040.

Lesson: benefits are front-loaded and diffuse; grid, water and tariff costs are local and long-lived. Social licence depends on data centres paying for, and bringing, their own resources.

1.3Research · Counterfactual

If they are not built, and if they are built badly

The asymmetry favours building, with discipline. Under-building costs strategic capability and value that are hard to recover. Over-building mostly costs investors, unless grid, water or subsidy costs are pushed onto everyone else.

Under-build: the demand goes elsewhere

  • Workloads move, not vanish: to Singapore (rationed), Johor, the Gulf or the US, though India is cheaper to build in (Mumbai ranks among the most cost-efficient markets).
  • Latency and compliance: payments, fraud scoring, health records and government AI cannot easily go offshore; they fall back on small, inefficient enterprise rooms.
  • Forex and value: cloud and AI paid for as imports; ~US$30 bn of domestic construction spend to FY30 lost.
  • Dependency: sovereign model training and government AI rely on foreign capacity that can be repriced, rationed or controlled.
  • GCCs: Bengaluru and Hyderabad lose their pitch against hubs with local GPUs.
  • No global environmental gain: the footprint shifts to other grids, often dirtier or drier (Malaysia ~0.74 kg CO₂/kWh; Johor water-limited).

Over-build or bad build: stranded and strained

  • Idle public compute: the IndiaAI portal had 144 users in Sept 2025 against 34,000+ GPUs (Takshashila); GPUs depreciate in 3–5 years whether used or not.
  • Speculative grid queues: 9.3 GW of the 26.3 GW projection has not even applied; Malaysia's draw was ~55% of approvals.
  • Obsolescence: only 25–30% of existing capacity can be upgraded for AI; halls built for 20–40 kW racks strand as racks pass 100 kW.
  • Bubble dynamics: Sify's CEO warns herd behaviour could create a glut; scholars compare it with the 2000s fibre overbuild.
  • Resource conflict: India's data-centre water is estimated at ~150 bn L (2025) rising to ~358 bn L (2030) (market-research estimate); stressed aquifers turn a national gain into a local cost.

1.4Guide · What matters

What matters, in order, and why

Power first, then demand certainty, then water and cooling, then design for density. In Bengaluru, water comes first.

Bengaluru: water before powerWhere the general framework bends.

  • Today: 31 of Karnataka's 32 private data centres sit in Bengaluru, mostly in the water-stressed east and south-east. WELL Labs estimates ~20 MLD (an upper bound; the atlas's central estimate is 3.4–4.5 MLD, chapter 12). Devanahalli draws groundwater at 169% of recharge.
  • The 1 GW goal on evaporative cooling would need ~34 MLD on site at 78% load; on dry liquid cooling, about 1 MLD (chapter 12).
  • The 2026–31 policy has the right architecture (PUE/WUE reporting, resource mapping, corridors outside the city) but weak price signals: the ₹8 lakh treated-water cap is ~₹3/kL for a 10 MW evaporative site.
  • Recommended: require treated or reclaimed water for new builds above a size threshold; set a WUE ceiling; keep latency-sensitive inference in the city and send GPU-dense training to corridors with surface water and round-the-clock renewables (Vizag, Jamnagar, Chennai); require renewable PPAs to add new supply, as Ireland now does.
  • Bengaluru's natural role: with 900+ GCC units and the deepest AI talent pool, low-latency enterprise inference and fine-tuning.

Where the sources disagree
  • "20% of data, 3% of capacity" is widely repeated, but MeitY calls the 20% unsupported and the 3% indicative.
  • Capacity baselines (1.5, 1.8, 1.9, 2.2 GW) differ by definition; the government keeps no official count.
  • 2030 forecasts run 4–12 GW (23 GW from an investment house); the 26.3 GW power-side figure is not comparable.
  • Jobs per MW span 0.15–188, from mixing construction, indirect and "ecosystem" jobs with permanent posts.
  • AI GDP claims are about adoption and do not isolate domestic data centres; national water totals come from a market-research firm, not metered data.
  • The Diffusion Rule's rescission was announced, but its formal removal may not have been published; the export regime remains fluid.
  • "Bubble unlikely" views come mainly from property brokers with a commercial interest.
