Cooling technology × heat rejection: what each combination really uses, and where it belongs

The questionA data centre's cooling has two layers: how heat leaves the server, and how it leaves the building. Which combinations work, what PUE and WUE do they reach in practice, and where and when should each be used in India?

The short answerThe heat-rejection plant sets the water; the cooling technology sets the temperature that decides which plant is possible. Warm-water direct-to-chip or immersion cooling with dry or adiabatic coolers gives the lowest total water and carbon in Bengaluru, even after counting the extra grid electricity. Open cooling towers save a few points of PUE but use the most total water at any Indian grid intensity: towers only break even where grid water exceeds about 9–18 L/kWh (chilled-water halls, against air-cooled or free-cooling chillers) or 35 L/kWh (direct-to-chip); India's grid is 2.9 (3.8 at the margin). Use them only on treated wastewater.

5.1Research · Two layers

Two layers, and which one drinks

Every data centre answers two questions: how does heat leave the chip, and how does it leave the site? Only the second usually evaporates water. But the first decides how warm the water is when it reaches the second, and that decides how often evaporation is needed at all.

Layer 1 · Cooling technology (server and room)

  • Room air: refrigerant (DX) units or chilled-water air handlers (CRAH)
  • Evaporative air: outside air through wet pads (direct), or air-to-air exchangers wetted on the outside (indirect, IEC)
  • Close-coupled: rear-door coils and in-row coolers fed by chilled water
  • Liquid: cold plates on the chip (direct-to-chip, DLC) at 30–45 °C; single- or two-phase immersion

Closed loops: almost no water. They set the supply temperature: 7–24 °C for air halls, 30–45 °C for liquid.

Layer 2 · Heat rejection (plant)

  • Dry: air-cooled condensers, air-cooled chillers (with or without free-cooling coils), dry coolers
  • Wet when hot: adiabatic or hybrid coolers that spray only on hot hours
  • Evaporative: open cooling towers, with or without chillers
  • Other sinks: seawater once-through, desalinated make-up, heat reuse

This is where water evaporates, and so it sets WUE. Evaporating 1 kWh of heat takes ~1.5 L plus blowdown.

5.3Research · In practice

What operators actually report

Fleet averages hide the mix of technologies and climates, but the "evaporative sites only" figures some operators publish are the best real-world anchor for cooling towers: about 1.2–1.6 L/kWh after optimisation, near 3 before.

What models saySame direction, similar size.

  • University of Michigan (2026), 200 MW site: water-cooled → air-cooled chillers cut direct water 97% (6.45 → 0.21 million m³) and raised electricity 32% (3.08 → 4.06 TWh); total water still fell 44% (10.93 → 6.12 million m³). A hybrid economiser had the lowest electricity, but economised only a quarter of the year.
  • LBNL (2024): US average PUE ~1.40 in 2023; average site WUE "just over 0.36 L/kWh".
  • PUE flatters air: liquid cooling removes server fans, which lowers IT power and can raise measured PUE even when total power falls. Compare total kWh per unit of compute, not PUE alone.
  • Water treatment matters as much as the plant: makeup = evaporation × cycles ÷ (cycles − 1). Going from 3 to 6 cycles cuts make-up ~20%; hard Indian borewell water caps cycles at 2–3 without softening or RO.

5.4Research · Climate

Which Indian cities can cool without chillers

The coldest a plant can make its water depends on the outdoor air: dry coolers follow dry-bulb, evaporation follows wet-bulb. Bengaluru has 5–6 °C more evaporative headroom than the coastal metros and the coolest dry-bulb peaks, which makes it India's best major hub for chiller-free warm-water cooling.

5.5Guide · Where and when

Where and when to use each combination

Decide by workload first (it sets rack density and the coolant temperature), then by water source, then by climate and land. In Bengaluru, run dry by default and add water only on the hottest hours, and only non-potable water.

Total water and carbon per MWh of ITBengaluru values. Bars: total water at the India grid's 2.9 L/kWh (on site dark, power stations light). The ranking holds at the marginal 3.8 and at every state mix.

Against free-cooling air-cooled chillers, a tower saves ~0.10 PUE but costs ~1.75 L/kWh on site, so it only wins on total water above ~17.5 L/kWh of grid water, far above any Indian figure. Its carbon edge is small: ~0.03–0.07 t CO₂ per MWh IT, and close to zero with renewable power.

By water source

  • Only drinking water or borewells: dry first (free-cooling chillers, DLC + dry, immersion). Adiabatic trim only with a metered cap, e.g. ≤0.3 L/kWh a year.
  • Treated wastewater (e.g. Hoskote's 60 MLD): adiabatic hybrids qualify; towers only with tertiary/RO polishing, ≥5 cycles and a Legionella plan.
  • Seawater (Mumbai, Chennai, Vizag, Jamnagar): once-through condensers with titanium exchangers; 28–31 °C sea limits it to warm-water loops; coastal permits dominate.
  • Land: dry coolers need ~2–3× the plant area of towers and are noisier; adiabatic pre-cooling shrinks the peak-day footprint.

Operating modes through the year

  • Liquid plant (W32): fully dry when dry-bulb ≤ ~26 °C (Oct–Feb nights, most monsoon hours); adiabatic sprays at ~26–35 °C (Mar–May afternoons), a few hundred to ~1,500 hours, ≈0.1–0.3 L/kWh; above ~35 °C or with no water, let supply float to 35–40 °C or run a trim chiller for the air share.
  • Air hall (20/30 °C chilled water): partial free cooling below ~28 °C dry-bulb (most of the year), full only below ~14–16 °C (rare). Raise supply air toward 26–27 °C: each +1 °C saves 2–5% of cooling energy (Singapore SS 697).
  • Existing towers: treated water only, ≥5 cycles, economiser when wet-bulb ≤ ~18–19 °C (~3,000+ h), or convert to hybrid coils and run towers as trim.

Beyond PUE and WUETrade-offs that decide real projects.

  • Legionella: open towers and adiabatic sprays are risks; adiabatic systems wet only for hours, which lowers but does not remove it. India has no data-centre code; adopt an ASHRAE 188-style plan.
  • Refrigerants: DX and chiller plants carry HFCs (R-410A GWP ~2,088; R-134a ~1,430); low-GWP options are R-32, R-454B, R-513A, R-1234ze. India's Kigali schedule freezes HFC use around 2028. Chiller-less liquid designs largely remove the issue; two-phase immersion fluids raise PFAS concerns.
  • Reliability: dry and adiabatic plants avoid depending on water supply, a real risk in Chennai 2019 and Bengaluru's tanker summers. Liquid loops add leak detection and CDU redundancy.
  • Heat reuse: DLC returns at 45–60 °C are reusable (Germany mandates it), but India's low heat demand makes this a future option: absorption chilling or industrial preheat.
  • Footprint and noise: dry coolers need more roof or land and run more fans; a consideration in dense Whitefield and Electronic City.
Where the sources disagree, and what is uncertain
  • WUE definitions differ (withdrawal vs consumption); fleet figures mix technologies and climates, and single-year values move a lot (NTT 0.72 → 0.38).
  • IS 7896 wet-bulbs may be 1–1.5 K low for Bengaluru and the coasts; Jamnagar's value is a misprint; Pune's official zone conflicts with common use.
  • Johor's WUE threshold appears as 1.8 and 1.4; China's limit as 1.25 and 1.3; Germany's 1.2 may become 1.3; Karnataka's WUE band is from a secondary tracker.
  • Most PUE/WUE values in the matrix are calibrated engineering estimates; IEC and immersion pPUEs are vendor case studies; no Indian measured value exists for any combination.
  • The atlas's 1.8 L/kWh tower value suits treated make-up; at 3 cycles on hard borewell water, towers may use 2.3–2.6 L/kWh.
