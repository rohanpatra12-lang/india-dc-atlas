Choosing power and cooling

The questionWhich cooling and power combination uses least water and carbon at an acceptable cost?

The short answerThe power source decides most of the water and all of the carbon; cooling decides the rest. Dry or liquid cooling with a solar–wind–battery mix uses a small fraction of the water of towers on grid power, and scheduling resolves most of the carbon–water conflict.

How this is calculated · cooling × power
  1. Average IT power (share of rated) L = idle share × (1 − compute utilisation) + busy power share × compute utilisation: a straight line between idle and busy power, as LBNL 2024 models servers
  2. IT energy (MWh/yr) = IT MW × L × 8,760
  3. Design overhead O = power-chain losses + other loads + cooling overhead (losses / other: 0.045 / 0.015 for every workload, the atlas standard), valid at the design load L₀ (conventional 70%, AI 80%)
  4. Part-load PUE = max(1.03, 1 + O × (φ × L₀ ÷ L + (1 − φ))), where φ is the load-independent share of overhead: other loads 100% fixed, power-chain losses 40%, cooling 25%. At low load the fixed part is spread over less IT energy, so PUE rises
  5. Cooling overhead depends on the city's design dry-bulb (DB) or wet-bulb (WB). Examples: towers 0.19 + 0.012(WB − 19.4); adiabatic dry coolers 0.24 + 0.01(DB − 24.6); air-cooled chillers 0.39 + 0.015(DB − 24.6); direct-to-chip + dry coolers 0.09 + 0.006 per °C above 24.6 (+0.02 if DB + 7 > 45). Calibrated so Bengaluru values equal the atlas cooling reference in Chapter 6.
  6. Facility energy = IT energy × PUE
  7. Direct water (ML/yr) = IT energy × WUE ÷ 1,000. WUE examples: towers 1.8 × (1 + 0.03(WB − 19.4)); indirect evaporative 0.55 × (1 + 0.07(WB − 19.4)); dry and liquid designs 0.01–0.10
  8. Water per kWh bought = cover × source water + (1 − cover) × state grid water. Remote power plants are divided by 0.92 for transmission losses
  9. Cover: physical (hourly) accounting uses the share of hours the source can actually supply (solar 35%, wind 55%, hybrid 70%, RTC 85%, least-water mix 83%, reservoir hydro 90%, run-of-river 60%, firm plants 100%); annual contract accounting credits 100% to the contracted source (except the least-water mix, which really buys 17% from the grid)
  10. Indirect water (ML/yr) = facility energy × water per kWh ÷ 1,000
  11. Price (₹/kWh) = generation + state open-access charge + ₹0.6 banking on the unmatched share; grid = DISCOM energy tariff. The demand charge (₹/kVA a month) is left out because it is the same whichever source supplies the energy; all-in tariffs are in Chapter 6.
  12. Annual cost (₹ cr) = facility MWh × price ÷ 10,000 + direct ML × water ₹/kL ÷ 10,000 + cooling capex × CRF(11%, 15 yr) + cooling O&M
  13. CO₂ (kt) = facility MWh × (cover × source factor + (1 − cover) × 0.675) ÷ 1,000
  14. Recommended combination: among mature, feasible designs (air-cooled designs for conventional, direct-to-chip for AI), pick the lowest total water whose annual cost is within the tolerance of the cheapest, powered by the least-water mix
  15. Water saved = selected total − recommended total; ₹ per kL saved = extra cost ÷ kL saved
Assumptions
  • Cooling overheads and WUE follow the atlas simulator and Report 12 ranges, checked against LBNL 2024, Uptime 2025, Munters case studies and NVIDIA/OEM rack specifications. They are engineering estimates for Indian design conditions, not measured site data.
  • Power-station water: coal 3.0 L/kWh for a new plant (MoEFCC 2015 norm; the atlas uses 3.5 for the fleet, the figure behind the India grid factor), dry-cooled coal 0.4, gas CCGT 0.75 and nuclear 2.6 (NREL medians), reservoir hydro 0/10/20 (switch), solar 0.1 (washing), wind ≈0. Grid at the meter: India 2.9 for every state (atlas standard; Karnataka's own mix 1.5 as a sensitivity).
  • Generation prices (₹/kWh, 2026): coal 5.5, dry coal 5.8, gas 9.0, nuclear 5.0, hydro 4.5, solar 2.7, wind 3.4, hybrid 3.2, FDRE 4.8, least-water mix 4.06 (from Chapter 16's ₹5.39 all-in with 17% grid at ₹7.0 and ₹1.0 open access). Open-access charges by state ₹1.0–1.6. Indicative, not quotes.
  • Cooling capex (₹ crore per MW of IT) 6–22, anchored to Chapter 15's cost stacks (about ₹10 crore/MW for colocation air cooling and ₹17 crore/MW for AI liquid cooling) and spread by Report 12's relative costs (air-cooled chillers −5%, indirect evaporative +20%, adiabatic +15% against tower plants). This sits inside the 18–28% mechanical share of ₹50–95 crore/MW builds (IMARC 2026; JM Financial 2025). O&M 3–5% of capex a year.
  • The least-water mix is Chapter 16's hourly optimisation on Karnataka-shaped solar and wind. Outside Karnataka it is indicative; its 83% clean share and ratios are carried over.
  • Idle and busy power: conventional servers idle at 36% of maximum power (2023) and run at 100% when busy; AI nodes idle at 20% of rated power and draw about 78% of rated when training (70% for mixed work). Default compute utilisation: conventional 30% (LBNL colocation 21–35%), AI training 80% (inference ~40%). Source: LBNL 2024 US Data Center Energy Usage Report, Section 3; Brookhaven measurement of an 8-GPU H100 node, 1.86 kW idle vs 8.4 kW peak (Latif et al., arXiv 2412.08602).
  • The fixed shares of overhead (100% / 40% / 25%) are engineering estimates for UPS no-load losses, minimum fan and pump speeds, lighting and security.
  • Demand (kVA) charges are left out because every option keeps a grid connection of similar size.

What the matrix says to do

  • Change the power before the cooling. On a state grid the indirect water (2.9 L/kWh) is usually larger than anything the cooling system evaporates, so the electricity source decides most of the total.
  • Clean is not automatically dry. Reservoir hydro (up to 20 L/kWh), nuclear and wet-cooled coal are all thirstier than the grid average. Solar, wind and storage are the water-light sources.
  • Idle servers still drink. A conventional server idles at about 36% of its maximum power, so at 30% compute utilisation nearly half of its electricity, and the water behind it, is used while it does no work (about 6% for an AI training cluster at 80%). Consolidating and virtualising servers is a water measure as well as an energy one.
  • Count water hour by hour. Under annual contract accounting a solar deal looks almost water-free, but at night the site still runs on grid power and grid water.
  • AI halls: direct-to-chip liquid with dry or hybrid coolers. It is the only design that is low on both energy and water in every Indian city in this tab.
  • Conventional halls: at a 5% cost tolerance the matrix picks zero-water air-cooled chillers in Bengaluru and the coastal metros, and adiabatic dry coolers in Hyderabad, Pune and Delhi NCR. Indirect evaporative cooling has the lowest running cost in the drier cities, and is worth choosing only on treated wastewater, never municipal water.
  • Avoid two-phase PFAS fluids for new builds until supply and regulation settle.

How this is calculated · hourly linear programme
  1. Decision variables: solar MW (Cs), wind MW (Cw), hydro contract MW (Ch ≤ 40), battery MWh (E); and for each of 8,760 hours hydro h, grid g, charge c, discharge d, state of charge q, curtailment u
  2. Energy balance, every hour: Cs·S(t) + Cw·W(t) + h + d + g − c − u = 65 MW
  3. Battery: q(t+1) = q(t) + 0.938·c − d ÷ 0.938 (88% round trip, cyclic); q ≤ E; c, d ≤ E ÷ 4 (4-hour unit)
  4. Hydro: h ≤ Ch each hour; monthly energy ≤ Ch × monthly capacity factor × hours (45% Jan … 85% Aug)
  5. Cost (₹ crore/yr) = Cs × (3.8 × CRF(10%, 25) + 1.5% O&M) + Cw × (7.0 × CRF + 1.5%) + Ch × ₹4.5/kWh take-or-pay + E × ₹0.105 cr/MWh + ₹1.0/kWh open-access charge on delivered RE + ₹7.0/kWh on grid energy
  6. Water (ML/yr) = 0.1 L/kWh × solar output + hydro L/kWh × hydro energy + 2.9 L/kWh × grid energy (India grid; the solve used 2.0)
  7. Frontier: minimise cost; minimise water; then minimise cost with water ≤ min + f × (cheapest − min), f = 0.75 … 0.05
  8. ₹ per kL saved = (cost − cheapest cost) ÷ (cheapest water − water)
  9. "Water cap N%": water allowed = minimum water + N% × (cheapest-mix water − minimum water)
Assumptions
  • Solar profile: Pavagada/Tumakuru-like, capacity factor 20.5%, monsoon dip. Wind: Chitradurga/Gadag-like, CF 33%, peaking June–August with an evening bias. Both synthetic (seeded random weather), not measured data.
  • Hydro water: base 10 L/kWh (reservoir evaporation), run-of-river 0, high 20. Grid 2.0 L/kWh in the solve; the atlas re-prices the grid share of every option at the India grid's 2.9 L/kWh and CEA's 0.675 t/MWh (the mixes themselves are unchanged). Solar 0.1 L/kWh (panel washing).
  • Flat 65 MW load (50 MW IT × PUE 1.30). CO₂ = grid MWh × 0.675 t (CEA); renewables counted at zero.
  • Solved with SciPy HiGHS; each solve ~35 s, 52,564 variables.

Three phases. First, hydro is squeezed out and the grid fills the gap: water falls 75% for 6 paise a kWh, but clean share drops from 79% to 70%. Then solar and batteries replace the grid: clean share climbs back to 83% and beyond. Finally only batteries can close the last night hours, and costs rise steeply.

  • ₹99/kL municipal benchmark is the BWSSB rate used in the atlas simulator; tanker water costs more, so the rule is conservative.

Up to the recommended option, every kilolitre saved costs less than tap water (₹85 on average). The next option averages ₹143 and the one after ₹249; at the margin, the extra kilolitres beyond the recommended point cost over ₹1,100 each. From there you pay more to save water than the water is worth.

Rule 2 · Pick a mix that survives the hydro uncertaintyAnnual water of each base-case mix if reservoir hydro really evaporates 0, 10 or 20 L/kWh. Nobody measures this for Indian reservoirs.

The cheapest mix leans on a 28 MW hydro contract, so its water could be anything from 361 to 2,754 ML. The recommended mix contracts no hydro: 303 ML whatever the true number is. When an input is this uncertain, a choice that does not depend on it is worth paying a little for.

Interpretation and the choice

  • Chosen: 161 MW solar, 86 MW wind, 379 MWh of 4-hour batteries, grid top-up, no hydro. ₹5.39/kWh, 303 ML a year (0.53 L/kWh), 83% clean, 64 kt CO₂.
  • Why this point: it is the lowest-water option whose water saving still costs less than municipal water (Rule 1); its water does not depend on the unmeasured hydro factor (Rule 2); and it cuts carbon as well as water (Rule 3).
  • Price: ₹10.5 crore a year more than the cheapest mix (+3.6%), yet ₹92 crore a year cheaper than all-grid power.
  • Hydro is not free water. At 10 L/kWh of reservoir evaporation a hydro PPA is more than three times thirstier than the India grid (2.9 L/kWh). If a genuine run-of-river contract exists (no reservoir evaporation), a mix with ~38 MW of it reaches ~173 ML at ₹5.30: the one case that beats this choice.
  • Rejected: the water-only knee (514 ML, ₹5.26) because it raises CO₂ to 113 kt; near-zero water (83 ML, ₹5.81) because each extra kL beyond the recommended point costs ~₹1,100; strict zero water (₹18.16/kWh) because it needs 593 MW of wind and 4.4 GWh of batteries.
  • On site, cooling still matters: this is the power side only. Pair it with dry or liquid cooling (chapters 4–6) or on-site water returns as the larger share.

16.3Model · Hourly carbon–water scheduling for an AI campus

Follow the sun for compute, unfollow it for evaporation

To cut carbon, run flexible AI work when solar power is plentiful. To cut water, avoid the hot afternoon hours when evaporative cooling drinks the most. Li, Yang, Islam and Ren (Communications of the ACM, 2025) call these two goals "not substitutable". This model plans one day hour by hour. It decides when deferrable work runs and whether the cooling sprays water or runs dry in each hour, and it shows how much of the conflict that second choice removes.

How this is calculated · hourly carbon–water scheduling
  1. Decisions each hour h: IT load cooled wet, ywet(h), and IT load cooled dry, ydry(h)
  2. Carbon(h) = (ywet·PUEwet + ydry·PUEdry) × CI(h)
  3. On-site water(h) = ywet·WUEwet(h) + ydry·0.03; grid water(h) = facility energy × EW(h)
  4. Minimise α × Carbon ÷ Carbonflat + (1 − α) × (k × on-site water + grid water) ÷ Waterflat, where "flat" is the baseline plan and k is the scarcity weight on local water
  5. Subject to: firm load every hour; total deferrable energy done within the day; ywet + ydry ≤ campus capacity
  6. Because each hour's cost is linear and the hours are linked only by the total-energy constraint, the exact optimum is: pick the cheaper mode in each hour, then fill deferrable work into the cheapest hours first (fractional knapsack)
  7. Chilled-water storage (optional): up to S MWh of IT heat in the most expensive hours is cooled with cold made at the coolest hour, with 5% storage losses; applied after the schedule is set
  8. Wet mode: PUE = 1.22 + 0.015 × max(0, DB − 25); WUE = 0 below 26 °C, else min(1.6, 0.1 + 0.107 × (DB − 26)). Dry mode: PUE = 1.22 + 0.035 × max(0, DB − 25); WUE = 0.03 (Chapter 8 adiabatic model)
  9. CI(h) = daily mean × (1 − dip × (s(h) − mean s)), with solar shape s(h) = max(0, sin(π(h − 6)/12))1.2; EW(h) = state grid water × CI(h) ÷ mean CI (grid water follows coal)
Assumptions
  • Hot-season design days (dry-bulb low / high): Bengaluru 22/35, Hyderabad 27/41, Mumbai 27/34, Chennai 28/38, Delhi NCR 28/43 °C. Illustrative, not typical-year weather.
  • Grid carbon averages 0.675 t/MWh in every state (CEA; one national grid); the midday solar dip is deepest in Karnataka (±40%), then Tamil Nadu (30%), Telangana (25%), Maharashtra (20%) and Uttar Pradesh (15%), reflecting their solar shares. Grid water at the meter: India grid 2.9 L/kWh at the meter (CEA publishes one national grid; carbon uses the same boundary).
  • Average IT load is 75% of capacity; the deferrable share is the part of daily energy that can move to any hour of the same day.
  • The adiabatic model is driven by dry-bulb temperature. In humid Mumbai and Chennai real sprays cool less, so wet mode saves less energy than shown.
  • Single site, one day, perfect forecasts. Real schedulers re-solve every hour on forecasts and can also move work between sites.

How to read the resultWhat the model shows, and what it leaves out.

  • Timing alone barely matters in India. When coal backs down at noon, both the grid's carbon and its water fall, so "follow the sun" helps both. The only part that rises in the afternoon is on-site evaporation.
  • Mode switching resolves most of the conflict. Running dry at midday raises PUE when the grid is cleanest, so the carbon cost of saving water is smallest exactly then. Spray only at night, when the air is cool and spraying is barely needed anyway.
  • Storage separates the two clocks. Chilled water made at night cools the hottest afternoon hours, so compute can follow the sun while evaporation does not.
  • Weight water by where it is taken. A litre evaporated on site in Bengaluru comes from a stressed city supply; a litre at a coal plant comes from a different basin. The scarcity slider sets how much more the local litre counts.
  • Limits: one site, one day, perfect forecasts. A production scheduler re-plans each hour on forecasts and can also move work between regions (Chapter 16; WaterWise).

What to do with it

  • Operators: give the cooling plant a "dry first" control mode with a water budget per day, and let the workload scheduler see both the hourly carbon forecast and the cooling plant's hourly water cost.
  • Tag work by flexibility. Training, fine-tuning, evaluation and batch inference can usually wait hours; interactive inference cannot. The deferrable share is what buys the savings.
  • Build in storage. A few hours of chilled-water storage is cheap next to the GPUs, and it lets the cooling plant spray only when the air is cool.
  • Regulators: CEA publishes an annual grid carbon factor but no hourly one, and no grid water factor at all. Hourly state factors for both, published day-ahead, would let every Indian operator run this kind of scheduler.
  • Report on a daily basis. Annual WUE hides the afternoon peaks that stress city supplies; ask for peak-day and peak-hour water alongside the annual figure.
  • Price water at its scarcity value. At Bengaluru's ₹99/kL tariff the model weights water lightly; at tanker prices or with a scarcity multiplier, dry-first operation pays for itself.
