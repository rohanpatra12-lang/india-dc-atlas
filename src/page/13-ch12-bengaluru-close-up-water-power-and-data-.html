Bengaluru close-up: water, power and data centres

The questionWhat does water cost in Bengaluru, where does it come from, how much would the city's data centres need if the coming capacity is conventional or AI, does rack density change that, and how could they run on treated sewage without leaving the city with stranded pipes?

The short answerBy volume, data centres are small users: even 1 GW on cooling towers would evaporate about 34 MLD, 1.7% of BWSSB's ~1,935 MLD, and AI halls on dry coolers would need under 1 MLD. By location they matter: most sit in groundwater belts drawn at 170–190% of recharge, and none are separately metered. Water costs about 2% of their power bill, so price will not change behaviour; siting rules, a WUE cap and shared, utility-owned treated-water mains will.

12.2Research · Sources

Where Bengaluru's water comes from

The Cauvery, pumped from ~100 km away, supplies most piped water. Borewells fill much of the gap. The one large source the city under-uses is its own treated sewage, most of which is sent to fill lakes in Kolar and Chikkaballapur.

Demand for fresh water is ~2,600 MLD against ~1,935 delivered by BWSSB, a gap of ~500 MLD filled by borewells and tankers (which mostly move groundwater). Of the delivered water, 26.5% is lost before it is billed.

12.5Model · Rack density

Does rack density change water or energy?

Not directly. Every kWh a server uses becomes a kWh of heat, and evaporating water to remove it takes ~1.5 L per kWh whatever the rack. What density changes is which cooling is possible: dense liquid-cooled racks run warm enough for dry coolers, and that is where the water saving comes from.

Rack density, water and energyWater per rack = kW × load × 24 × WUE. Pick a heat-rejection path to see that water per MWh stays flat on towers while water per rack climbs with density.

Six ways density matters indirectly

  1. Feasibility: above ~30–50 kW air is impractical; NVL72 racks need liquid at the chip.
  2. Coolant temperature: NVIDIA specifies 45 °C inlet water (ASHRAE W45 class).
  3. No chillers: 45 °C water can be cooled by outside air until ~35 °C ambient, which covers almost all of Bengaluru's year.
  4. Fan and compressor energy: liquid removes server fans and room air handlers, so PUE falls from ~1.25–1.45 to ~1.10–1.15.
  5. Free-cooling hours: Bengaluru's mild wet-bulb (~19.4 °C) suits towers too, which is why the choice is close here.
  6. Concentration: 1 GW needs ~125,000 racks at 8 kW but ~7,600 at 132 kW, so demand lands on a few big meters: easier to supply with treated water, harder on one local aquifer.

The trade in one line

The same NVL72 hall uses ~4.3 kL per rack per day on towers and ~0.13 kL on dry coolers: a ~97% cut in on-site water for ~4% more electricity. Because power-station water is 2.9 L/kWh on the India grid, that 4% adds back only a fraction of what is saved. Bengaluru's summer peaks (mid-30s °C) should be checked against hourly ISHRAE/IMD data before claiming chiller-free operation.

12.6Model · Treated wastewater

Running data centres on treated sewage

Bengaluru treats far more sewage than any data-centre fleet could use. But secondary-treated water must be polished before it can go into a cooling tower, and a dedicated pipe has to be paid for. At full cost, tertiary water is about as expensive as drinking water.

What has to be builtFrom the treatment plant to the tower.

  1. Source. BWSSB offers secondary water (BOD ~25, ammonia ~35, suspended solids ~35 mg/L). Towers need ammonia below ~1–5 mg/L and phosphate below ~1–5: ammonia above 0.5 corrodes copper and feeds biofilm.
  2. Tertiary treatment. Filtration or membrane bioreactor → ultrafiltration → partial or full reverse osmosis (RO) → UV or chlorine. RO rejects ~25% of its feed.
  3. On-site polishing. Side-stream filters, softening, biocide dosing and a Legionella plan (ASHRAE 188). Polished water allows 6–8 cycles of concentration instead of ~3, halving make-up.
  4. A separate purple pipe. Pumping station at the plant, a DN300–500 main for 1–5 MLD, 2–3 days of storage on site.
  5. Disposal. RO reject plus blowdown (~30–35% of feed, salty) back to the sewer under pollution-board consent. Zero liquid discharge works but costs a lot of energy.
  6. Fallback. A metered, capped potable or borewell connection for plant outages, curtailed in drought.

Energy: UF + RO ~0.8–1.2 kWh/m³ and pumping ~0.1–0.5 kWh/m³ add only ~0.2–0.3% to a campus's electricity.

12.7Policy · Pipes that outlive their customers

When a data centre closes, and the other policy questions

Servers are replaced every 4–6 years, buildings last 15–25, pipelines 30–50 and park leases 30+30. A pipe built for one customer is a stranded asset waiting to happen; a shared, utility-owned non-potable main is not.

Design so a closure is a non-eventWho owns what, who else can use the water, and who pays if a customer leaves.

ToolHow it works
Shared trunk, private branchBWSSB or KIADB owns a looped non-potable main along a corridor; each data centre pays only for its branch and meter. On closure, only the branch is capped.
Second usersOther KIADB industry; construction (demand jumped to ~6 MLD in 2024 when potable use was banned); parks and landscaping; lake top-up; district cooling for nearby offices; recharge structures.
Take-or-payPay for 70–80% of reserved capacity for the loan term (10–15 years), used or not.
Capacity reservation feeA charge on unused allocation, so the 60 MLD at Hoskote is not hoarded.
Successor clauseObligations attach to the KIADB lease and pass to the next occupant; data-centre buildings are usually re-let.
Exit payment and bondUndepreciated branch and dedicated plant paid on early exit; a bond of ~10–15% of branch capex covers capping and restoration.
Step-in rightThe utility can take over an on-site tertiary plant when a tenant leaves.

Who carries what: the utility carries the trunk, priced into a common tariff; the customer carries its branch and dedicated plant through take-or-pay and exit payments; the state carries drought reallocation through published curtailment rules.

The policy packageEight steps, in the order they unlock each other.

  1. Meter and disclose. A "data centre" category at BWSSB and KSPCB; monthly withdrawals by source (Cauvery, borewell, tanker, treated); site PUE and WUE published, as the 2026–31 policy now asks.
  2. Hydrology first. In over-exploited taluks, approve new sites only with treated water or dry cooling; no new groundwater NOCs for cooling in Bengaluru Urban or Devanahalli.
  3. A performance standard. For example WUE ≤0.3 L/kWh on potable or groundwater, towers only on tertiary water. Fix the policy's 0.7–1.3 incentive band, which leaves out the best (sub-0.7) designs.
  4. Full-cost treated water. Price tertiary water near cost (~₹60–90/kL) but below potable. Turn the ₹8 lakh/yr reimbursement cap into capital support for shared mains, where the real cost lies.
  5. Shared non-potable grids with the contracts on the left, starting at Hoskote (KC Valley tap) and Whitefield (Kadubeesanahalli).
  6. Drought rules. A pre-agreed ladder: in a declared drought, data centres switch to dry or adiabatic mode or cut load before households lose supply.
  7. Reject and blowdown. Consent specifies the disposal route and salinity limits; prefer sewer return over energy-hungry zero discharge.
  8. One window. IT department, KIADB, BWSSB, KSPCB, the groundwater authority and GBA in a single approval desk, with a public register of who gets treated water.

Fairness: treated water already fills 274 lakes in Kolar and Chikkaballapur, and KC Valley delivers only ~290 of its 440 MLD design. Selling it to data centres is fair only if it replaces drinking water or groundwater and is priced at full cost. Hoskote's 60 MLD is ~2.7× what a 500 MW AI park on towers would need and ~100× a dry-cooled one; tie the allocation to the cooling design.
